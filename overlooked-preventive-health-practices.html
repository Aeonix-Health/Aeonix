Two Preventive Health Habits Almost Everyone Skips

2026-09-25  ·  6 min read

Most preventive care conversations default to blood panels and cancer screening. Two other categories carry real, well-documented evidence behind them and are consistently under-addressed — not because the evidence is weak, but because neither feels like "medical" prevention in the way a screening test does.

Adult vaccination — coverage is lower than most people assume

Vaccination is usually framed as a childhood topic, but several vaccines recommended for adults have strikingly low uptake. CDC's own 2024 National Health Interview Survey data shows Tdap (tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis) coverage among adults at just 28.6%, despite broad recommendation. Shingles vaccination (recombinant zoster vaccine, 2 doses) among adults 50 and older has improved from 1.1% at its 2018 introduction to 28.7% by 2024 — real progress, but still leaving roughly seven in ten eligible adults unprotected. Pneumococcal vaccination coverage actually decreased among adults 65 and older in recent tracking, even as the CDC broadened its recommendation in October 2024 to include all adults 50 and older, not just those 65-plus.

The pattern across all three: recommendation exists, benefit is well-established, and uptake still lags — largely because adult vaccination isn't built into the same recurring-appointment structure that, say, an annual physical is.

Sleep — treated as a lifestyle choice, not a clinical variable

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the CDC both recommend at least 7 hours of sleep per night for adults, and the American Heart Association's own cardiovascular health framework includes sleep duration as one of its core metrics. The evidence behind this isn't soft: a large meta-analysis of 43 studies found a U-shaped mortality curve, with both short sleep (under 7 hours) and long sleep (over 8-9 hours) associated with higher all-cause mortality risk, and shorter sleep durations specifically linked to elevated blood pressure and impaired blood vessel function.

Sleep rarely comes up in a checkup unless a patient raises it, yet it functions as an upstream risk factor for exactly the conditions preventive screening is trying to catch downstream — hypertension, metabolic dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease.

Why these two, specifically

Both categories share a structural reason for being overlooked: neither requires a lab test to identify, so neither generates the kind of "result" that prompts a follow-up conversation. Bringing them up explicitly — checking your own vaccination record against current recommendations, and treating consistent sleep duration as seriously as diet or exercise — costs nothing and closes a real, measurable gap.
