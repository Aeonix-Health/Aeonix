What's Actually Included in a Complete Preventive Health Exam?

2026-09-25  ·  6 min read

"Complete" is doing a lot of work in that phrase, and it means different things depending on who's using it. A standard annual physical and a comprehensive preventive exam share a starting point but diverge sharply from there. Here's what each one actually covers.

What a standard annual physical covers

According to guidance from the American Academy of Family Physicians, a standard well exam typically includes a medical and family history review (covering genetic risk factors for heart disease, diabetes, and cancer, substance use, and mental health symptoms), a physical assessment (height, weight, BMI, vital signs including heart rate and blood pressure, and a visual exam of the eyes, chest, abdomen, and extremities), and age- and sex-appropriate screening — for example a PSA discussion for men or a Pap smear and mammogram referral for women, alongside routine screening for hypertension, obesity, and depression.

This is a genuinely useful visit. It is also, deliberately, brief — most primary care physical exams are built to fit a 15-to-30 minute appointment slot and a small number of billable, guideline-mandated items.

What "comprehensive" should actually mean

A comprehensive preventive exam extends the standard visit along two axes: breadth of biomarkers tested, and depth of interpretation. Where a standard check-up might draw a basic metabolic panel and a total cholesterol reading, a comprehensive panel adds markers like ApoB, Lp(a), fasting insulin, hsCRP, and a fuller hormonal and thyroid picture — markers that exist in the same blood draw but simply aren't ordered by default because population guidelines don't mandate them for asymptomatic, average-risk adults.

The distinction matters because absence of a symptom is not the same as absence of risk. Population screening guidelines, discussed in more depth in our piece on which preventive screenings you actually need, are calibrated to catch the highest-burden conditions at population scale — not to give any one individual a complete read of their own biology.
