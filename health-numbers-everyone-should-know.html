The Handful of Numbers Everyone Should Actually Know About Their Own Body

2026-09-25  ·  5 min read

You don't need to memorize a full lab report. The American Heart Association narrows the essentials down to a small set of numbers that, taken together, capture most of your cardiometabolic risk — the category of risk most closely tied to the leading causes of death and most responsive to early action.

The core numbers, and what the ranges mean

MarkerGenerally healthy range
Blood pressureBelow 120/80 mmHg
HbA1c (blood sugar, 2–3 month average)Below 5.7%; 5.7–6.4% is prediabetes; 6.5%+ is diabetes range
Body Mass Index (BMI)18.5–24.9 (18.5–22.9 for Asian populations)
Waist circumferenceUnder 88cm (women), under 102cm (men) — lower thresholds for those of Asian ancestry

The American Heart Association is explicit that cholesterol is part of this picture too, but doesn't reduce it to one number the way it does with blood pressure — total cholesterol, LDL ("bad"), HDL ("good"), and triglycerides each carry distinct meaning, and a physician's interpretation of the full lipid profile matters more than any single cutoff.

Why these specific numbers

Each one flags a different failure mode that tends to be silent until it isn't. Blood pressure is sometimes called the "silent" risk factor precisely because it produces no symptoms while doing damage. Elevated HbA1c can precede a diabetes diagnosis by years, during which the window for reversal through diet and activity changes is far wider than after diagnosis. BMI and waist circumference are imperfect proxies for body composition, but they correlate with meaningfully elevated risk of high blood pressure, heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and sleep apnea once they cross established thresholds.

Where this fits with the rest of your health picture

These four numbers are a floor, not a ceiling — they're what the AHA considers non-negotiable to track, not the full picture of long-term health risk. Family history changes how aggressively any one of these should be managed, and a single reading tells you less than a trend across two or three consecutive checks. Knowing where you stand on all four, and how they're moving, is a reasonable minimum bar before your next physical.
