Swiss Workplace Stress Is at a Record High. Here's What It Does to Your Body.

2026-04-06  ·  8 min read

Burnout is still, in most clinical settings, managed as a purely psychological condition — assessed with questionnaires, treated with therapy and rest. What's missing from that picture is the biology. Chronic workplace stress leaves a measurable blood signature, and in Switzerland right now, more employees are showing that signature than at any point since national tracking began. That signature carries a real cost: population studies tie chronic stress to measurably shorter life expectancy.

Swiss workplace stress, by the numbers

Switzerland's most recent Barometer Gute Arbeit (Working Conditions Barometer) — an annual survey by Travail.Suisse and the Bern University of Applied Sciences, now in its 11th year, published in November 2025 — found that 42.4% of Swiss employees suffer from stress often or very often, and 41.1% feel emotionally exhausted at the end of a workday. Both figures are the highest recorded since the survey began in 2015.

A peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis published in Swiss Medical Weekly, covering 23 Swiss studies, put the pre-pandemic baseline prevalence of clinical or severe burnout at roughly 4% of the working population, with milder but still clinically significant burnout affecting around 18% — and the trend since that academic baseline has moved consistently upward, not down. The biology of burnout: what to look for

Cortisol (fasting, morning). In early burnout, cortisol is typically elevated. In advanced burnout — after months or years of chronic stress — the HPA axis becomes depleted and cortisol paradoxically falls below normal. A morning cortisol below 300 nmol/L in a symptomatic adult warrants further investigation.

DHEA-S. Dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate is produced by the adrenal glands and typically declines in burnout. Low DHEA-S alongside elevated or depleted cortisol is a classic adrenal stress pattern.

hsCRP and IL-6. Chronic stress drives systemic inflammation. Elevated hsCRP and IL-6 in the context of burnout symptoms indicate that the inflammatory load is contributing to fatigue, mood changes, and cognitive impairment.

Thyroid function (full panel). Subclinical hypothyroidism — particularly low free T3 — is both a consequence and accelerator of chronic stress. TSH alone is insufficient.

Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR. Chronic cortisol elevation drives insulin resistance. Many burnout patients have a metabolic component that is entirely unaddressed.

Why a blood test adds something a questionnaire can't

A stress or burnout questionnaire captures how you feel right now. It can't tell you whether your cortisol rhythm has flattened, whether your DHEA-S is depleted, or whether systemic inflammation is quietly driving your fatigue and brain fog. Those are measurable, physician-interpretable facts — and they point toward specific interventions (nutritional, hormonal, anti-inflammatory) rather than "rest more and see a therapist" as the only available answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is burnout officially recognised as an illness in Switzerland?

Burnout itself is not classified as a stand-alone occupational disease under Swiss law; it's generally addressed clinically through related diagnoses such as adjustment disorder or depressive episodes. That classification gap is part of why reported prevalence varies between studies. The underlying biological changes are directly measurable regardless of how the condition is officially classified.

Can a blood test diagnose burnout on its own?

No. It can't replace a clinical or psychological assessment, but it can measure the biological signature of chronic stress — cortisol and its daily rhythm, DHEA-S, hsCRP, and IL-6 — helping a physician distinguish ordinary tiredness from a genuine, measurable stress response and track it over time.

What is the single most useful marker for assessing burnout?

There isn't one. Morning cortisol and DHEA-S together give the clearest picture of HPA axis function, while hsCRP and IL-6 reveal how much systemic inflammation is contributing to fatigue and cognitive symptoms. Looking at any single marker in isolation risks missing the overall pattern.
