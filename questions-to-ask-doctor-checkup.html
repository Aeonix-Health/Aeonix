The Questions Most People Forget to Ask at Their Checkup

2026-09-25  ·  5 min read

The average primary care visit is short, and most of it is consumed by the standard exam. If you walk in without a plan, it's easy to leave with your original concern addressed but nothing else covered. A checkup is one of the few times a year you have a qualified physician's undivided attention — treating it as a conversation to prepare for, rather than an appointment to simply show up to, changes what you get out of it.

Use a framework, not memory

The U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) — the federal agency responsible for improving the quality and safety of healthcare — built a free tool for exactly this problem: the AHRQ Question Builder, which helps patients assemble a written question list before any type of appointment, including routine checkups. The evidence-backed logic behind it is simple: patients who write questions down in advance ask more of them, and get more complete answers, than patients who try to recall everything in the room.

AHRQ also promotes what it calls the SHARE Approach for shared decision-making — a model built specifically around screening and preventive-care decisions, where the benefits, harms, and trade-offs of a given test or intervention are meant to be discussed explicitly rather than assumed. That framing is useful to borrow even informally: for any screening your doctor recommends or you're considering, it's reasonable to ask what it's for, what a positive or negative result would actually change, and what happens if you skip it.

Questions worth having ready

  • "Given my age, sex, and family history, which screenings am I actually due for right now?" — recommendations shift by decade, and it's easy for a routine checkup to miss one that applies specifically to you.
  • "Are there any of my results trending in a direction worth watching, even if each one is individually still 'normal'?" — this is the trend-over-single-value point that matters for most biomarkers.
  • "Is anything I'm taking — prescription, over-the-counter, or supplement — worth reconsidering at this point?" — a periodic medication review benefits people well before senior age.
  • "What would change your recommendation if I skipped this test?" — this surfaces whether a screening is genuinely decision-relevant for you or a default recommendation.
  • "What's the most useful thing I could do differently before we meet again?" — a direct way to get one clear, prioritized action rather than a generic list.

Bring data, not just questions

The conversation is only as good as the information behind it. Walking in with your own recent biomarker history — rather than relying on whatever's in the clinic's own records, which may be incomplete if you've tested elsewhere — lets your physician actually engage with trend data on the spot, rather than treating each visit as an isolated snapshot.
