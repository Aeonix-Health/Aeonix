Burnout Has a Blood Test: What Stress Looks Like on a Lab Report

2025-01-15  ·  6 min read

Swiss workplace stress and exhaustion are at record levels: the most recent Barometer Gute Arbeit (November 2025) found that 42.4% of Swiss employees suffer from stress often or very often, and 41.1% feel emotionally exhausted at the end of a workday — both the highest figures recorded since the survey began in 2015. A peer-reviewed meta-analysis in Swiss Medical Weekly, covering 23 Swiss studies, puts baseline clinical or severe burnout at around 4% of the working population, with milder but still clinically significant burnout affecting roughly 18%. The clinical debate around burnout's precise definition continues — but the biology is increasingly clear.

What burnout looks like in blood

Burnout is not a single hormonal event. It unfolds in stages, each with a distinct biological signature:

Stage 1 — Activation: Cortisol is elevated, particularly in morning readings. The HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis is in overdrive. DHEA-S — the cortisol counterpart that promotes resilience — begins to decline. hsCRP may begin to rise as the sustained stress response drives inflammation.

Stage 2 — Resistance: The adrenal glands struggle to maintain output. Cortisol may appear "normal" but shows a flattened diurnal curve — lacking the sharp morning peak that characterises healthy HPA function. Testosterone continues to decline (cortisol and testosterone are inversely related). Sleep quality deteriorates.

Stage 3 — Exhaustion: Cortisol can paradoxically fall below normal. DHEA-S is markedly depleted. Inflammatory markers — hsCRP, IL-6, and TNF-α — are significantly elevated. Thyroid function may be suppressed (rT3 rises, T3 falls).

Why this matters for treatment

The intervention for Stage 1 burnout is fundamentally different from the intervention for Stage 3 burnout. In Stage 1, reducing HPA stimulation (stress reduction, sleep repair, adaptogenic support) is appropriate. In Stage 3, the priority is adrenal recovery — which requires a different approach, including careful nutritional support and, in some cases, medical management.

Treating Stage 3 burnout as if it were Stage 1 — by adding more stimulants, pushing harder, expecting recovery to come from willpower — is both ineffective and biologically damaging. The blood tells you which stage you are in.

Frequently asked questions

Is Switzerland's burnout rate actually the highest in Europe?

No reliable evidence supports that. A peer-reviewed meta-analysis in Swiss Medical Weekly found Swiss burnout prevalence "comparable with those available in other countries" — 18% overall burnout, 4% clinical or severe. What is well documented is that Swiss workplace stress and exhaustion are rising domestically, with 2025 figures the highest recorded since tracking began in 2015.

Can a blood test diagnose burnout on its own?

No. It can't replace a clinical or psychological assessment, but it can measure the biological signature of chronic stress — cortisol and its daily rhythm, DHEA-S, hsCRP, and IL-6 — helping a physician distinguish ordinary tiredness from a genuine, measurable stress response and track it over time.

What's the difference between the three stages of burnout?

Stage 1 (Activation) shows elevated cortisol and early inflammation. Stage 2 (Resistance) shows a flattened cortisol rhythm and declining sleep quality. Stage 3 (Exhaustion) shows depleted cortisol and DHEA-S alongside significantly elevated inflammatory markers and suppressed thyroid function. Each stage calls for a different intervention.
